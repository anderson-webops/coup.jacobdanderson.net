Expanded study · 12 October 2026

Think about the opponent first

The new study tunes a finite set of policies, selects contenders, then tests them on untouched seeds. Targeted counters and component removals receive separate panels.

Bluffs have a price

Tax bluffs pay well when nobody challenges. Selective challengers change that calculation. Own cards and revealed cards can make a claim impossible.

Survival changes choices

Challenge risk, assassination, defensive cards, and threat targeting depend on remaining influence. A last-influence victim can also attempt a Contessa bluff block.

Leaders can be countered

A policy that beats one named target may perform poorly in other fields. The counter panel tests that narrower question separately.

Small leads stay unresolved

Rankings show observed rates. A stronger conclusion needs the preselected comparison and its uncertainty bounds. The strict simultaneous bounds often leave close matchups open.

Testing and reproducibility

A larger study with separate stages

Every action in the new study is checked for card conservation, valid influence, nonnegative coins, and elimination state. Independent tests also check information boundaries and reproducibility.

Search without reusing confirmation

24 parameter configurations are tuned first. Screening compares six selected tuners, seven richer named strategies, and eleven original controls. The union of the top two in each matchup plus three controls advances without an arbitrary cap.

Two uncertainty methods

Preselected paired differences use approximate simultaneous normal bounds and conservative Hoeffding bounds across 35 primary comparisons. A positive normal bound alone is labeled approximate evidence; a strict bound crossing zero leaves the comparison unresolved.

Public information only

New strategies estimate card availability from their own hand and revealed cards. Claim credibility and action values are heuristic assumptions. Learning uses public events and resets every game. Strategy decisions use a separate random stream from the deck.

Independent verification

All 2,814,728 saved outcomes were independently audited. A second checker reconstructed 26,912 games from the plans and matched every winner and turn count. The automated suite has 122 tests covering rules, policy information boundaries, statistical audits, publication, and release integrity.

Limits that remain

This is finite policy research, not an optimality proof or a human-play forecast. The simulator uses clockwise response priority, an unlimited Treasury, and automatic proof of held cards. It omits negotiations and expansions.

Expanded study policy guide

What the newer bots try

The names below describe decision rules, not personalities or proven winners. Their exact parameters are in the downloadable research bundle.

Card cautious

Uses visible card counts and selective challenges, makes only held-card action claims, and avoids ordinary bluff blocks. It can still make a last-chance Contessa claim when assassination would end the game.

Card balanced

Uses card availability and threat scoring with occasional action and block bluffs.

Card bold

Tries bluffs and block bluffs more often and accepts more challenge risk.

Public learning

Updates a rough opponent reliability estimate from public proofs and failed claims, and reacts to the table's challenge rate.

Eliminator

Gives assassination a higher action score when it can be afforded.

Defensive

Values keeping Contessa more highly when opponents can afford assassination, while bluffing less.

Exchange builder

Tries Ambassador exchanges more often when it lacks Duke, then keeps cards using the current table state.

Legal random

Samples a legal action on each turn, including a Coup from seven coins; its other decisions still follow fixed rules.

The rulebook model

Claims, challenges, and costs

Every game uses the standard five-role, fifteen-card court deck. Two influence cards are dealt to each player.

01

One action per turn

Income gives 1 coin, Foreign Aid gives 2, Tax claims Duke for 3, and a Coup costs 7. A player starting a turn with at least 10 coins must Coup.

02

Claim a role or bluff

Assassin pays 3 to attack, Captain steals up to 2, and Ambassador exchanges with the court. A truthful claim survives a challenge and still resolves after the challenger loses influence.

03

Blocks can be challenged

Duke blocks Foreign Aid, Contessa blocks assassination, and Captain or Ambassador blocks stealing. A proven block holds; a disproved block fails.

04

The Assassin's fee

A successful Contessa block leaves the 3-coin fee spent. A successful challenge to the Assassin claim cancels the action and refunds the fee if the actor remains in play.

Rules checked against the base Coup rulebook PDF and Dized Coup rules.

Initial study policy guide

What the original bots do

These eleven heuristics Coup as soon as they have seven coins; the rulebook requires a Coup only at ten. After two failed actions, they take guaranteed Income once. The expanded policies above use different choices.

Baseline

Claims only held roles. Prioritizes Duke tax, then Captain steals, occasional Ambassador exchanges, and Foreign Aid. Challenges impossible claims.

Income

Takes guaranteed Income until a Coup is affordable.

Foreign Aid

Seeks 2 coins per turn, subject to Duke blocks.

Taxer

Claims Duke tax on every non-Coup turn, including as a bluff.

Captain

Claims a steal whenever another player has coins, whether or not it holds Captain.

Assassin

Claims assassination at 3 or more coins and uses Foreign Aid to fund it.

Exchange

Sometimes exchanges when holding Ambassador without Duke; otherwise follows the truthful baseline.

Bluff heavy

Claims Tax without checking its hand and claims blocks whenever possible.

Challenge low

Uses baseline actions; sometimes challenges Assassin, Captain, or Contessa claims.

Challenge heavy

Uses baseline actions and challenges every role claim.

Block aggressive

Uses baseline actions but claims any available block, even without the card.

Evidence and limits

How to read these numbers

The simulator rotates a distinct focal player through every seat. Each candidate in the same matchup uses the same initial seed panel; mixed opponents cycle across positions.

Win rate

Wins divided by all scheduled games, including games that reached the 1,000-action cap. Draws have their own column.

Uncertainty

Table intervals are 95% Wilson intervals for a policy's win rate. Finding cards use paired differences for the four preselected top-two pairs, with a simultaneous 95% correction.

Scope

Policies use only private cards and public information. The model omits negotiation, optional variants, and the choice to decline proof of a held role. Response priority is clockwise when several players could act.

Two players

The first seat starts with one coin under the base two-player rule. View those exploratory results separately from standard multiplayer games.

Reproduce or inspect

The source, per-game outcomes, seat rows, study manifest, and rule citations are kept together. Prior invalid CSVs are clearly archived outside the active results.
